# Reflective AI Fluency: Manifest (draft 1)

Nov 3, 2026 · Sebastian Nicolajsen

Reflective AI Fluency is the ability to work with AI effectively, efficiently, ethically and safely in your own work, and to keep getting better at it. It builds on the 4D AI Fluency Framework by Rick Dakan and Joseph Feller (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) and adds what that framework leaves implicit: practising it in one's own context, growing the scope of what AI helps with, and the reflective quality that makes practice compound.

## What we believe

Fluency is something you do, in your own work, and it shows in how you adapt. Nine statements set the stance.

1. **Fluency is behaviour, not knowledge.** Knowing the 4Ds matters only if it changes what people do with AI on a Tuesday afternoon.
2. **Fluency lives in context.** It is learned on your own tasks, in your own domain, because that is where you can tell a good answer from a wrong one.
3. **Judgement comes before speed.** Checking, questioning and noticing what is missing are the habits most people skip, so they come first.
4. **Polish is not proof.** The more finished AI output looks, the more deliberately it must be checked.
5. **No mode is the goal.** Augmentation, automation and agency are tools; fluency is choosing the right one for the task and changing when it stops working.
6. **Scope grows with trust.** People hand AI larger pieces of work only as their ability to judge the result keeps up.
7. **Fluent people get better on their own.** They look for what they are missing, notice, adjust and carry lessons forward; that quality is what separates fluency from competence.
8. **Some work stays human on purpose.** Fluency includes knowing what you should keep doing yourself, to protect judgement, expertise or responsibility.
9. **Good enough is the default trap.** AI answers anything fluently and rarely shows you what you missed, so people settle for the first acceptable result. Fluent people go looking for the better one.

## The four competencies

We keep Dakan and Feller's four competencies unchanged and express each as a question a person asks about their own work.

| Competency | In plain words | The question in your own work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delegation | Deciding whether, when and how to use AI, and in which mode | Should AI help with this step, and how much should it do? |
| Description | Communicating goals, context, format and how the AI should work | Have I told it what I would tell a capable new colleague? |
| Discernment | Judging output, reasoning and the collaboration itself | How would I know if this is wrong, incomplete or misleading? |
| Diligence | Taking responsibility: choosing tools well, being open about AI's role, verifying before use | Can I stand behind this, and would I be comfortable saying how it was made? |

All four apply in every mode and at every level; what changes is the size of the work they govern.

## Reflective fluency

Reflection is not a fifth competency or a separate loop; it is the quality with which all four are performed. A fluent person works like a fluent speaker: they notice a misunderstanding and rephrase mid-sentence, without stopping to run a procedure.

It shows in four ways:

- **Curiosity.** Looking for what a better result would be, even when the current one seems fine: asking for alternatives, comparing approaches, trying another mode.
- **Noticing.** Recognising, during the work, that output is drifting, a check is missing or the mode is wrong.
- **Adjusting.** Changing the description, the check or the mode in response, including trying a different mode on a task you already know well.
- **Carrying forward.** Turning what worked into reusable practice (a prompt, a checklist, a workflow) and revising it when it fails.

While fluency is still developing, reflection is often deliberate and visible; with fluency it becomes part of how the work is done. Fluency is reached when the adjustment happens without being asked for.

Its opposite is **satisficing**: stopping at the first result that is good enough. With AI this is the default, not a character flaw. The output always looks acceptable, the model rarely signals what is missing, and good-enough use already saves time, so there is no felt reason to improve. The cost is real but invisible: errors no one sees and value no one reaches. Curiosity is the counterweight.

## Four levels of scope

Levels describe how much of a person's work the competencies govern, not how sophisticated they are. The behaviours stay the same; their object grows from a single answer to a task, a workflow and finally an agent's run.

| Level | What the person does | Moves up when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curious | Asks AI ad hoc questions, often one-shot, often accepts the first answer | They iterate on a real task and check at least one claim that matters |
| Task | Reliably improves recurring tasks in their own domain | Their checks are habitual and they start reusing what works |
| Workflow | Designs repeatable, reviewed processes; decides where AI does not belong | Their workflows run with review points and they can define boundaries |
| Agentic | Directs AI to work independently within boundaries, including for others | — |

Each competency, anchored per level:

| | Curious | Task | Workflow | Agentic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Delegation | Tries AI on a question | Should AI help with this task? | Which steps go to AI, which stay human? | How much autonomy, within which boundaries? |
| Description | A question | A prompt with goal, context and format | A workflow specification | An agent brief with boundaries and checkpoints |
| Discernment | Notices obvious errors | Checks facts, questions reasoning, spots gaps | Places review points in the process | Judges behaviour across runs, not single outputs |
| Diligence | Knows not to share sensitive data | Verifies before sharing; is open about AI's role | Builds verification and disclosure into the process | Answers for outputs that others rely on |

Moving up depends on Discernment: scope grows only when the ability to judge the result keeps pace.

## Moving between modes

The three modes come from Dakan and Feller and are not ranked; fluency is matching the mode to the task and switching when it stops fitting. Three questions decide the mode: is the goal clear, can I check the output, and does the task recur?

| | Augmentation | Automation | Agency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Is the goal clear? | Not yet: explore together to find what good looks like | Yes | Yes |
| Can I check the output? | Partly | Yes, quickly | Yes, through checkpoints |
| Does it recur? | Not yet | One-off or occasional | Often, with bounded risk |

Start in augmentation. Move to automation once you can describe and check the result, and to agency once the task recurs and boundaries are set. Step back down when output drifts or the stakes rise.

The common mistake is the reverse path: newcomers start with one-shot automation before they know what good looks like. Exploring a new mode is safest on a task from your own domain, where you can still judge the result.

## The behaviours we look for

We use Anthropic's 24 behavioural indicators as the core and add the reflective quality as a lens across them. Eleven indicators can be seen in AI conversations; thirteen happen outside them.

**Observable in conversation** (with how often they appeared in Anthropic's AI Fluency Index, Feb 2026):

| Behaviour | Competency | Index |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iterates and refines | Description | 85.7% |
| Clarifies the goal before asking for help | Delegation | 51.1% |
| Provides examples of what good looks like | Description | 41.1% |
| Specifies format and structure | Description | 30.0% |
| Sets how the AI should work (interaction mode) | Description | 30.0% |
| Communicates tone and style | Description | 22.7% |
| Identifies when AI might be missing context | Discernment | 20.3% |
| Defines the audience for the output | Description | 17.6% |
| Questions reasoning that doesn't hold up | Discernment | 15.8% |
| Consults AI on approach before execution | Delegation | 10.1% |
| Checks facts and claims that matter | Discernment | 8.7% |

**Outside the conversation** (13 indicators; wording to be confirmed against Anthropic's tutorial):

- **Delegation:** deciding whether AI should be used at all; choosing a suitable tool and mode; splitting work between person and AI.
- **Discernment:** judging whether the collaboration is working; judging the AI's behaviour, not only its output.
- **Diligence:** considering privacy, bias and ethics before use; being open about AI's role; verifying before sharing; owning the consequences.

**Reflective quality** (our addition, applied to all of the above): curiosity, noticing, adjusting and carrying forward, as defined earlier. It is evidenced by change over time, for example a check that becomes routine or a mode that is switched when output drifts.

We also watch for the opposite: satisficing (stopping at the first acceptable output on work that matters), accepting output passively, delegating vaguely, and trusting polished output without checking.

## Fluency in your own work

Fluency is always fluency in something: it shows in how a person handles the steps of their own work.

For any step of their work, a fluent person can answer four questions:

| Question | Shows |
| --- | --- |
| Which mode, or no AI, and why | Delegation, including choice of mode |
| How I would know the output is wrong | Discernment |
| What role AI plays in the result (consultant, tool builder, cocreator, medium) | Diligence and openness about AI's role |
| Whether this step should stay human on purpose | Protecting expertise and judgement |

Over time, the answers change: new modes, new checks, and steps joined into larger pieces of work. That change is what growing fluency looks like.

## What this is not, and open questions

Reflective AI Fluency is not a tool course, not a prompt-technique catalogue and not a ranking of people. It does not treat automation or agency as the goal, and it does not certify knowledge about AI.
