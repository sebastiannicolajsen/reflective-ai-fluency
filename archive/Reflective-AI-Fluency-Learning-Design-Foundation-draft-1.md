# Reflective AI Fluency: Learning Design Foundation (draft 1)

Nov 4, 2026 · Sebastian Nicolajsen

This is the support document for the design-partner skill. It is written against manifest draft 5 and replaces the earlier learning theory draft. It does not design a programme. It states how exercises, learning goals and learning journeys for Reflective AI Fluency should be designed, why, and how strong the evidence for each reason is.

The body is written for the skill: plain reasons, no theory names. Section 9 is for the designer only (names, sources, verification status). The skill never shows peers the theory behind a rule, only the reason and how confident we are.

## 1. How the skill uses this document

1. **Ask before judging.** Before giving feedback, the skill asks what the exercise is meant to change, who it is for, which ways of working it covers, and which real task it uses. It also asks whether the peer is an experienced facilitator or new to learning design, and adjusts how much structure it offers (rule 1 applies to the skill's users too).
2. **Lead with what matters most.** It gives the two or three points that most change the design, not a full checklist.
3. **Quote the exercise.** It points to the text of the exercise it is reacting to.
4. **State confidence in plain words.** Each rule carries a rating:
   - **Established:** peer-reviewed, replicated or meta-analytic evidence, possibly from a neighbouring field.
   - **Plausible:** strong evidence from a neighbouring setting that has not been tested on AI-assisted work.
   - **Preprint:** AI-specific evidence that is not yet peer-reviewed.
   - **Our judgement:** a design choice with no direct evidence. The skill says so.
5. **Name one strength only when it is specific.** It does not give a total score, and it does not praise by default. AI reviewers of learning designs tend to over-praise and agree with experts only moderately, so the skill treats its own alignment and depth judgements as tentative.
6. **Return the decision.** It ends with a question the designer decides.
7. **Behave fluently itself.** It shows its reasoning, says what it could not check, and prompts the peer to look back at what they changed.

## 2. What the design has to produce

The manifest defines the outcomes. A design is good when it develops:

- **Behaviour in the person's own work**, not knowledge about AI.
- **A per-step choice of way of working** (single interaction, collaboration, workflow, agency), including the choice to use no AI.
- **The five quality dimensions:** reliability, proportionate checking, reasoning, adaptation, transfer.
- **Reflective quality:** curiosity, noticing, reviewing, adjusting, carrying forward, evidenced by change between two runs of the same task with a stated reason.
- **Quality that matches autonomy and stakes.** The more autonomy and the higher the stakes, the more quality the design must build before it asks people to work that way.

## 3. The seven layers

A design answers one question per layer. Layers 0 to 6 run in this order, and two checks run across all of them (section 5).

| Layer | The question the designer answers | What it takes from the framework |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0. Starting point | Who is this for, and how experienced are they in each way of working? | Way of working × dimension |
| 1. Outcome | What will people do differently, on which step, in which way of working? | Behaviour in own work |
| 2. Evidence | What trace will show it, and could AI produce that trace alone? | Traces, two runs |
| 3. Practice | What do people actually do to build it? | Ways of working, dimensions |
| 4. Feedback and reflection | How do they find out what to change? | Reflective quality |
| 5. Transfer and environment | What makes the first real use at work happen? | Own context, recurrence |
| 6. Evaluation | How will we know, later, that work changed? | Traces over time |

## 4. Design rules by layer

Each rule has: **Why** (plain reason), **Evidence** (plain summary and rating), **Does not hold when**, and **Skill asks**.

### Layer 0: Starting point

**Rule 1. Match support to experience in this way of working, and withdraw it as people gain skill.**
- **Why:** Step-by-step help speeds up people who are new to a way of working and slows down people who already know it. Experience belongs to a way of working, so one person can be expert in collaboration and new to workflows.
- **Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 60 experiments (N = 5,924) found that beginners gained from high support and experienced learners did better with less. Established; the samples were mostly students.
- **Does not hold when:** Seniority is used as a stand-in for experience. When unsure, give support: the evidence suggests that holding it back from a beginner costs more than giving it to an expert.
- **Skill asks:** "Who in the group already uses workflows well, and who is new to them?"

### Layer 1: Outcome

**Rule 2. State each outcome as a behaviour in a named way of working, including choosing not to use AI.**
- **Why:** An outcome phrased as understanding can be met without any change in how someone works. A named way of working says what the person decides and where, and "no AI" is a decision as much as "agent".
- **Evidence:** Follows from the framework. Studies of workplace training find that skills based on judgement transfer much more strongly when conditions at work support them (89 studies). Starting design from observable on-the-job behaviour is a standard practitioner method, but we found no controlled test of it. Plausible; the wording of outcomes is our judgement.
- **Does not hold when:** A short awareness session only aims to inform. Then label it as awareness and do not call it fluency.
- **Skill asks:** "What will the participant do differently, on which real step, in which way of working?"

### Layer 2: Evidence

**Rule 3. The evidence of learning must be a trace of the person's own work and judgement that AI cannot produce alone.**
- **Why:** If an agent can finish the exercise convincingly, finishing it proves nothing. A rule that merely states how AI may be used does not change what people do; the task itself has to make the use visible.
- **Evidence:** In one field test, AI-written exam answers went undetected in 94% of cases and outscored a random set of real students in 83.4%. Assessment research argues that stated rules without structural change leave validity unprotected. Established for the problem; example patterns (such as comparing an AI answer with one's own) are preprint.
- **Does not hold when:** The task is practice and not evidence. Practice tasks do not need to be AI-proof. Also, evidence locked down so tightly that it hides how the person used AI defeats the purpose.
- **Skill asks:** "If someone gave this exercise to an agent, what would we still see of their own judgement?" The skill also runs a generic prompt through the exercise and reports what came back.

**Rule 4. Ask for two runs of the same task and the reason for what changed.**
- **Why:** One finished output can be made by AI. A difference between two attempts, with a reason, is harder to fake and shows learning.
- **Evidence:** Thin. It follows from the framework's definition of reflection. Process evidence is more resistant to AI than a single artefact, but nobody has validated it as a measure of AI-practice quality, and reflections can be written by AI as easily as essays. Our judgement.
- **Does not hold when:** The person was already working well and has no reason to change. No change and no reason means no evidence, not failure.
- **Skill asks:** "What would someone actually see differing between the two runs?" A reason without a checkable difference does not count.

### Layer 3: Practice

**Rule 5. Build practice on whole tasks from the person's own work, and vary them.**
- **Why:** Judgement develops when it is practised whole and in real conditions. Invented tasks teach invented judgement.
- **Evidence:** A meta-analysis of programmes designed around whole tasks found large gains (d = 0.79), mostly in education and technical training. A meta-analysis of critical-thinking instruction (341 effects) found a smaller average gain (g = 0.30) that grew with authentic problems, discussion and mentoring. Established for education; plausible for consulting.
- **Does not hold when:** Someone lacks a basic mechanic, such as how to set up a workflow tool. A short drill first is fine. Confidentiality can also rule out real tasks; then use realistic ones the person recognises.
- **Skill asks:** "Which real task from these people's week could carry the exercise?"

**Rule 6. Let people meet and fix AI errors on purpose, with a clear signal of what went wrong.**
- **Why:** People who practise finding and recovering from errors handle new situations better than people who only practise correct procedures.
- **Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 24 studies (N = 2,183) found a moderate advantage (d = 0.44) that was largest on structurally new tasks (d = 0.80). Established, but 21 of the 24 studies taught software or database search.
- **Does not hold when:** Feedback is unclear: the effect was not significant there, and that describes much agentic work. Planted errors that are too easy teach people to hunt for plants. Where the error is invisible, add a checkpoint that reveals it.
- **Skill asks:** "How will participants know what they missed?"

**Rule 7. Show good and poor examples of AI use, including a case where using no AI was the right call.**
- **Why:** Contrast makes the decision visible. Good examples alone teach imitation. And AI use is not always the better choice: across 106 experiments, human-AI combinations did on average worse than the better of human or AI alone, and worse on decision tasks.
- **Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 117 workplace training studies found that models showing both good and poor practice transferred better, mostly for interpersonal skills. The human-AI finding is from a Nature Human Behaviour meta-analysis (g = −0.23). Established for both; the design implication is our judgement.
- **Does not hold when:** True beginners are shown a poor example before any good one (our judgement).
- **Skill asks:** "Is there a case here where leaving AI out is the right answer, and does the exercise reward that choice?"

**Rule 8. Mix decisions between ways of working in the same exercise.**
- **Why:** The hard part is telling which way fits a step, and that is only practised when the options sit side by side.
- **Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 59 papers (N = 8,466) found a moderate benefit from mixing similar categories (g = 0.42, 0.29 after bias adjustment), with no reliable benefit for learning from text. Plausible analogy; nothing tests it for choosing among ways of working.
- **Does not hold when:** The way of working is new to the group, or the content is reading material.
- **Skill asks:** "Would it help to mix steps where different ways of working compete? This is a reasoned bet."

**Rule 9. Use discussion of real cases, led by someone who works fluently in that way.**
- **Why:** Saying why you chose a way of working or a check, and hearing a challenge, builds reasoning.
- **Evidence:** In the critical-thinking meta-analysis, discussion, authentic problems and mentoring each helped, and the combination helped most (a secondary summary reports g = 0.57 for 19 studies; verify before quoting). Established for education; plausible for consulting. That the coach should be fluent in the way of working is our judgement.
- **Does not hold when:** One voice dominates the group.
- **Skill asks:** "Who leads the discussion, and have they used this way of working on real work?"

### Layer 4: Feedback and reflection

**Rule 10. End every practice round with a structured debrief that names one change for next time.**
- **Why:** A few minutes of structured looking back reliably improves the next attempt, and it produces the stated reason that rule 4 needs.
- **Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 46 samples (N = 2,136) found that debriefs improved performance by about 25% over controls (d = .67), more when facilitated and structured. Established.
- **Does not hold when:** The debrief is an unstructured "how did it feel" or turns into blame.
- **Skill asks:** "What will the debrief ask, and who records the change?"

**Rule 11. Use structured reflection on complex outputs, not on everything.**
- **Why:** Stopping to test a first judgement helps on hard cases. On easy ones it costs time and adds little.
- **Evidence:** In medical diagnosis, deliberate reflection helped on complex cases and not on simple ones. A meta-analysis of five studies of guided reflection found a small gain (g = 0.365), and several student studies were null. Established in medicine; plausible for AI work.
- **Does not hold when:** The output is simple or routine, or when the aim is better calibration (rule 12).
- **Skill asks:** "Is this output complex enough to justify a reflection step?"

**Rule 12. Add outside feedback so people can judge how well they check.**
- **Why:** Reflecting makes people perform better. It does not reliably tell them how good their checking is.
- **Evidence:** In a dermatology study, deliberate reflection raised diagnostic accuracy and left confidence and calibration unchanged. An AI-use experiment (N = 704, preprint) found that feedback on how people had used the assistant changed their behaviour and left measured metacognitive accuracy unchanged. Established in medicine; preprint for AI.
- **Does not hold when:** Not applicable; this rule states a limit.
- **Skill asks:** "How will participants learn whether their checks matched what an error would have cost?"

**Rule 13. Give feedback on the work and the next step, not on the person.**
- **Why:** Feedback about the person tends to backfire; feedback about the task and what to do next tends to help.
- **Evidence:** Reviews of feedback research find large average effects with wide variation, and one major review found more than a third of feedback interventions lowered performance, especially those aimed at the self. Established; spot-check the figures before quoting.
- **Does not hold when:** Not applicable.
- **Skill asks:** "Does the feedback say where they are going, how they are doing, and what to try next?"

**Rule 14. Make it safe to report what the AI got wrong, and say who hears it.**
- **Why:** People who fear looking careless hide AI errors, and hidden errors cannot be learned from.
- **Evidence:** Studies of work teams link psychological safety to learning behaviour. Established in general; no AI-specific study found.
- **Does not hold when:** Errors involve client harm or confidentiality; then the reporting route must be set before the session.
- **Skill asks:** "Where can people say what the AI got wrong, and who hears it?"

### Layer 5: Transfer and environment

**Rule 15. Plan the first real use before people leave: when, on which task, with whose support.**
- **Why:** Most learning is lost at the first return to work. A concrete if-then plan and a manager who expects the new behaviour make the first use likely.
- **Evidence:** Across 642 tests, implementation plans had an average effect of d = .36, larger when people were motivated and rehearsed the plan. Workplace training meta-analyses link transfer to a supportive environment, and the link is stronger for open skills such as judgement than for closed skills such as software. Established, with the environment findings correlational.
- **Does not hold when:** The behaviour is a whole style of judgement. If-then plans suit discrete actions, such as "if I hand a step to an agent, I first write the checkpoint".
- **Skill asks:** "What is the first real task where they will use this, and does their manager know?"

**Rule 16. Bring the same task type back after a gap, so that a second run is possible.**
- **Why:** The two-run trace needs a second run, and spacing helps retention.
- **Evidence:** Spacing is well established in memory research. Evidence for spaced judgement training in professionals is thinner. Plausible.
- **Does not hold when:** The task type will not recur in the person's work.
- **Skill asks:** "When does this task come up again, and how will the second run be captured?"

### Layer 6: Evaluation

**Rule 17. Judge by traces of changed work over time. Collect usefulness ratings if you collect ratings at all.**
- **Why:** Liking a session says little about whether work changed.
- **Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 34 studies (115 correlations) found that "this was enjoyable" ratings related weakly to learning and later behaviour, and that usefulness ratings related more strongly to later behaviour. Established. No validated measure of AI-practice quality exists: in a review of 22 studies covering 16 AI-literacy scales, 13 scales were self-report and 3 were performance-based.
- **Does not hold when:** A quick usefulness rating is fine as an early signal.
- **Skill asks:** "What trace will show the change a month later, and who sees it?"

## 5. Cross-layer checks

**Alignment.** The outcome, the practice and the evidence ask for the same behaviour, and the AI-use condition is stated together with the evidence that shows how AI was used. Patterns to name:
- The outcome says "judge" or "choose", the task only asks people to list.
- People are assessed on something they never practised.
- AI could complete the task without the target judgement.
- The AI-use condition does not match the outcome: a task that bans AI cannot evidence an outcome about using it, and a task that allows anything cannot evidence an outcome about knowing when to leave it out.
- A change over time is assessed from one artefact.
- Activities serve no outcome, or outcomes have no practice or evidence.

Evidence: design logic, not trials. Two critiques argue that used as an audit it becomes box-ticking and narrows learning. The skill uses it as a check, with an alignment matrix of outcomes against tasks, AI-use condition and evidence. Rating: plausible.

**AI stress-test.** Run a generic prompt through the exercise. If the result would satisfy it, the exercise tests the AI. Re-run it when models change, because the result goes out of date. Rating: established for the problem (rule 3).

## 6. How the design rules map to the quality dimensions

| Dimension | Design elements that plausibly build it | Rating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reliability | Meeting and fixing errors on purpose (6); poor examples (7); reflection on complex outputs (11) | Established in neighbouring fields; analogy for AI |
| Proportionate checking | Cost-of-error cases (our judgement); outside feedback on whether checks caught what mattered (12) | Thin |
| Reasoning | Discussion of real cases (9); structured debriefs (10) | Established in education and teams |
| Adaptation | Varied whole tasks (5); error practice (6); mixing decisions (8) | Established for lab tasks; plausible for AI |
| Transfer | Own-work tasks (5); first-use plans and manager involvement (15); return after a gap (16) | Established, with environment findings correlational |

Calibration, meaning how well people know how reliable their own judgement is, sits under proportionate checking. No reviewed intervention reliably trains it.

## 7. Limits: where this is our judgement

The skill says so plainly when a peer asks about any of these.

- How to train the per-step choice of way of working.
- How to train proportionate checking and calibration.
- How to train supervision of agents. No study was found.
- How to diagnose starting points per way of working, and how to sequence the ways.
- Whether the five dimensions work as a set. Each has separate support, and nobody has tested them together.
- Whether the two-run comparison measures reflection or only shows a behaviour change.
- How people decide per step whether AI is worth its cost in tokens, time and review effort.

## 8. What this replaces

For the designer, not the skill:

- **Constructive alignment** moved from spine to cross-layer check.
- **SOLO** and **semantic waves** moved to private vocabulary. SOLO grades the structure of a written response and agreement is mixed; semantic-wave evidence comes from schools and universities, and no trial with working adults was found. The useful design move, going from principle to the learner's own case and back, is better supported by the evidence for authentic problems and discussion (rule 9).
- **The zone of proximal development** gave way to the evidence on support that helps beginners and hinders experienced learners (rule 1).
- **70-20-10 percentages, andragogy as justification, productive failure** (conceptual goals only) and **deliberate practice** beyond "practice with feedback" are dropped or downgraded.
