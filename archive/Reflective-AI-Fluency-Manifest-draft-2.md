# Reflective AI Fluency: Manifest (draft 2)

Nov 4, 2026 · Sebastian Nicolajsen

Reflective AI Fluency is the ability to work with AI effectively, efficiently, ethically and safely in your own work, and to keep getting better at it. It builds on the 4D AI Fluency Framework by Rick Dakan and Joseph Feller (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) and adds what that framework leaves implicit: practising it in one's own context, widening the scope of what AI helps with, and the reflective quality that makes practice compound.

The framework has two axes. **Scope** is how much of your work the four competencies govern. **Quality** is how well and how reflectively you perform them at that scope. The two are linked: the wider the scope, the higher the quality needed to work safely.

## What we believe

Fluency is something you do, in your own work, and it shows in how you adapt. Nine statements set the stance.

1. **Fluency is behaviour, not knowledge.** Knowing the 4Ds matters only if it changes what people do with AI on a Tuesday afternoon.
2. **Fluency lives in context.** It is learned on your own tasks, in your own domain, because that is where you can tell a good answer from a wrong one.
3. **Judgement comes before speed.** Checking, questioning and noticing what is missing are the habits most people skip, so they come first.
4. **Polish is not proof.** The more finished AI output looks, the more deliberately it must be checked.
5. **No mode and no scope is the goal.** Augmentation, automation and agency are tools; so is the size of the work you hand over. Fluency is choosing the right one for the task and changing when it stops working.
6. **Scope grows with judgement.** People hand AI larger pieces of work only as their ability to judge the result keeps up.
7. **Fluent people get better on their own.** They look for what they are missing, notice, adjust and carry lessons forward; that quality is what separates fluency from competence.
8. **Some work stays human on purpose.** Fluency includes knowing what you should keep doing yourself, to protect judgement, expertise or responsibility.
9. **Good enough is the default trap.** AI answers anything fluently and rarely shows you what you missed, so people settle for the first acceptable result. Fluent people go looking for the better one.

## The four competencies

We keep Dakan and Feller's four competencies unchanged and express each as a question a person asks about their own work.

| Competency | In plain words | The question in your own work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delegation | Deciding whether, when and how to use AI, and in which mode | Should AI help with this step, and how much should it do? |
| Description | Communicating goals, context, format and how the AI should work | Have I told it what I would tell a capable new colleague? |
| Discernment | Judging output, reasoning and the collaboration itself | How would I know if this is wrong, incomplete or misleading? |
| Diligence | Taking responsibility: choosing tools well, being open about AI's role, verifying before use | Can I stand behind this, and would I be comfortable saying how it was made? |

All four apply in every mode and at every scope; what changes is the size of the work they govern.

## Axis 1: Scope

Scope is the object the four competencies govern: a single answer, a task, a workflow, a system of workflows. The behaviours stay the same; the object grows. Scope is about the size of the work, not about how independently the AI performs it; that is the mode (Augmentation, Automation, Agency), which can occur at any scope.

Scope has a **difficulty gradient but no value gradient**. Wider scope is harder to do well: more steps to check, longer chains where errors compound, a higher cost of an undetected error. It is not better. Someone who works excellently at task scope is not behind someone running a system badly, and some work should stay at a narrow scope or fully human on purpose.

| Scope | The object | What the person does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Answer | One response | Asks AI questions, often one-shot, and judges the answer |
| Task | A recurring piece of work | Improves a task in their own domain, with prompts, context and checks |
| Workflow | A chain of steps | Designs repeatable processes with review points; decides which steps stay human |
| System | Several workflows, or work that others rely on | Owns how AI is used across connected workflows, including for others |

Each competency, anchored per scope:

| | Answer | Task | Workflow | System |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Delegation | Decides whether a question is worth asking AI | Should AI help with this task? | Which steps go to AI, which stay human? | Where does AI act, and where is it kept out, across the workflows? |
| Description | A question with enough context to be answerable | A prompt with goal, context and format | A workflow specification | Specifications, boundaries and checkpoints that hold across workflows |
| Discernment | Notices obvious errors; checks a claim that matters | Checks facts, questions reasoning, spots gaps | Places review points in the process | Judges behaviour and drift across workflows and over time, not single outputs |
| Diligence | Does not share sensitive data | Verifies before sharing; is open about AI's role | Builds verification and disclosure into the process | Answers for the system and for outputs that others rely on |

## Axis 2: Quality

Quality is how well and how reflectively the four competencies are performed at a given scope. It is assessed within a scope, not across scopes.

### Reflective quality

Reflection is not a fifth competency or a separate loop; it is the quality with which all four are performed. A fluent person works like a fluent speaker: they notice a misunderstanding and rephrase mid-sentence, without stopping to run a procedure.

It shows in four ways:

- **Curiosity.** Looking for what a better result would be, even when the current one seems fine: asking for alternatives, comparing approaches, trying another mode.
- **Noticing.** Recognising, during the work, that output is drifting, a check is missing or the mode is wrong.
- **Adjusting.** Changing the description, the check or the mode in response, including trying a different mode on a task you already know well.
- **Carrying forward.** Turning what worked into reusable practice (a prompt, a checklist, a workflow) and revising it when it fails.

While fluency is developing, reflection is often deliberate and visible; with fluency it becomes part of how the work is done.

Its opposite is **satisficing**: stopping at the first result that is good enough. With AI this is the default, not a character flaw. The output always looks acceptable, the model rarely signals what is missing, and good-enough use already saves time, so there is no felt reason to improve. The cost is real but invisible: errors no one sees and value no one reaches. Curiosity is the counterweight.

### Observable traces of reflection

Reflection cannot be seen in a snapshot. It is a difference between two points in time on the same task. We look for these traces:

- A prompt, check or workflow that changed between versions, with a stated reason.
- A check added after an error was caught.
- A mode switched when output drifted or stakes rose.
- Alternatives requested after an acceptable answer (the anti-satisficing trace).
- A reusable artifact revised after it failed.
- The person can name what the AI got wrong last time and what they do differently now.

**Method:** compare two runs of the same task and ask one question: "What did you change since last time, and why?"

**Rule:** no change and no reason means no evidence, not failure. A stable practice that still works is not a lack of reflection.

### How the axes link

- **Curiosity pushes, Discernment permits.** Curiosity drives people toward wider scope. Discernment decides whether the move is safe. Curiosity without the ability to judge output only widens the scope of unchecked work.
- **Required quality rises with scope.** The same weak check that is harmless on one answer is dangerous in a system others rely on.
- **Stepping down is quality.** Being able to own a system is scope. Knowing when to step back from it, or to reduce the AI's autonomy within it, is quality.

The axes give four positions, and the first is the most common risk:

| | Narrow scope | Wide scope |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Low quality** | One-shot answers, accepted unchecked | Workflows or systems running with no real checks |
| **High quality** | Sharp interrogation of single answers and tasks | Wide delegation with matching checks, boundaries and revision |

## Moving between modes

The three modes come from Dakan and Feller and are not ranked; fluency is matching the mode to the step and switching when it stops fitting. The decision is made **per step**, not per task, and autonomy is capped by **checkability × cost of an undetected error**.

| Question | If no |
| --- | --- |
| Is the goal clear? | Augmentation: explore with AI to find what good looks like |
| Can I check the output, quickly or at checkpoints? | Augmentation, or keep the step human |
| What does an undetected error cost, and is it reversible? | If high and irreversible: AI drafts, human decides |
| Does it recur or have volume? | Don't invest in building automation; a one-off agentic run is fine if the first three answers are yes |

Recurrence decides whether building automation is worth the effort. It does not decide whether delegating is allowed.

**Step-down triggers:** output drifts, stakes rise, or you can no longer say how you would know it is wrong.

The common mistake is starting with one-shot automation before you know what good looks like. Exploring a new mode is safest on a step from your own domain, where you can still judge the result.

## The behaviours we look for

We use Anthropic's behavioural indicators as the core (24 in total) and apply the reflective quality as a lens across them. Eleven can be seen in AI conversations; thirteen happen outside them.

**Observable in conversation:**

| Behaviour | Competency |
| --- | --- |
| Iterates and refines | Description |
| Clarifies the goal before asking for help | Delegation |
| Provides examples of what good looks like | Description |
| Specifies format and structure | Description |
| Sets how the AI should work (interaction mode) | Description |
| Communicates tone and style | Description |
| Identifies when AI might be missing context | Discernment |
| Defines the audience for the output | Description |
| Questions reasoning that doesn't hold up | Discernment |
| Consults AI on approach before execution | Delegation |
| Checks facts and claims that matter | Discernment |

**Outside the conversation** (13 indicators; wording below is a grouping, not Anthropic's exact text):

- **Delegation:** deciding whether AI should be used at all; choosing a suitable tool and mode; splitting work between person and AI.
- **Discernment:** judging whether the collaboration is working; judging the AI's behaviour, not only its output.
- **Diligence:** considering privacy, bias and ethics before use; being open about AI's role; verifying before sharing; owning the consequences.

We also watch for the opposite behaviours: satisficing (stopping at the first acceptable output on work that matters), accepting output passively, delegating vaguely, and trusting polished output without checking.

## Fluency in your own work

Fluency is always fluency in something: it shows in how a person handles the steps of their own work.

For any step of their work, a fluent person can answer four questions:

| Question | Shows |
| --- | --- |
| Which mode, or no AI, and why | Delegation, including choice of mode |
| How I would know the output is wrong | Discernment |
| What role AI plays in the result (consultant, tool builder, cocreator, medium) | Diligence and openness about AI's role |
| Whether this step should stay human on purpose | Protecting expertise and judgement |

Over time, the answers change: new modes, new checks, steps joined into larger pieces of work. That change is the trace of growing fluency.

## What this is not

Reflective AI Fluency is not a tool course, not a prompt-technique catalogue and not a ranking of people. It does not treat automation, agency or wider scope as the goal, and it does not certify knowledge about AI.
