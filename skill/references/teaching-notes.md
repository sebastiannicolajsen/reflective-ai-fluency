# Explaining the framework to someone new

Read this first when the person is new to the framework, when you are unsure how much they know, or whenever an answer would otherwise lean on terms like "agent", "workflow", "calibration" or "trace". Assume they do not know them.

Nothing here adds a claim to the framework. It shows how to make the existing claims understandable. The claims themselves are in the other reference files.

## How to explain

1. **Start from a situation they recognise, then name the idea.** "You have a 40-page client report and a Friday pitch" comes before "ways of working".
2. **Use one running example for the whole answer.** Switching examples makes the reader rebuild the picture each time. The example below works for most questions.
3. **Define each term the first time it appears,** in plain words, in the same sentence, with a short example. Never leave a term for the next paragraph.
4. **Give the reason as what goes wrong without it.** "Checking matches the cost of an error" is a rule. "If the summary invents a growth forecast and you send it to the client, the client acts on a number nobody said" is a reason.
5. **Three points at most, each developed.** A reader keeps three points that are explained, and none of seven that are compressed. Do not pack rules into chains of semicolons or brackets.
6. **When asked how to design something, show a concrete sketch first** (task, steps, what the facilitator does, what you would see), then name the principle behind it.
7. **End by letting them apply it.** One question about their own case, which they can answer in a sentence.

## The running example

A consultant has a 40-page annual report from a client's competitor and a pitch on Friday. The question is which parts of the work AI should help with, and how. This is an illustration of the framework, not a recommendation for every report.

| Way of working | What it looks like here | Who sets the path | When you would notice an error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| No AI | You read the section on the competitor's strategy yourself, because you will be questioned on it in the room. | You | Immediately, because you did it |
| Single interaction | You paste one section, ask for the key figures, read the answer, and check the numbers against the page. | You, in one step | When you read the answer |
| Collaboration | You and the AI go back and forth: "what risks does it mention?", "which claim is weakest?". You steer as you learn what matters. | You and the AI, step by step | As each answer comes |
| Workflow | You do this for 30 reports every quarter. You write the steps once (pull the figures, compare with last year, flag changes), the AI runs them on each report, and you check at fixed points. | You, in advance | At the fixed checks |
| Agency | You say "find reports on our competitors and flag anything that changes our pitch", set limits (which sources, nothing gets sent), and the AI decides what to read and in what order. | The AI, within your limits | Only at checkpoints and in the result |

What differs is who decides the path, what you can inspect, and how late you would see a mistake. None is better in itself. The summary above for a one-off pitch probably needs the single interaction or the collaboration. The workflow earns its setup effort only because the task repeats.

## Terms in plain words

Use these wordings, or similar ones, with the example in brackets.

- **Way of working.** How the work with AI is arranged: who decides the next step. (You ask one question, or the AI plans its own route.)
- **Agent, or agency.** The AI decides its own steps within limits you set. You see the goal and the result, not each step, so a mistake can sit in a step you never saw. (It reads twelve reports and drops the one with the bad news.)
- **Workflow.** You fix the steps in advance, the AI carries them out. You can inspect each step. (Pull figures, compare, flag.)
- **Checkpoint.** A place in a longer run where it stops and shows you something before going on. (After reading, it lists which pages it used.)
- **Proportionate checking.** Checking hard where a mistake would be costly, lightly where it would not. (You verify the revenue figure in a client memo and skim a draft agenda.)
- **Calibration.** How well your confidence matches how often you, and the AI, are actually right. (Over ten summaries, how many times did you catch it being wrong? If you never looked closely, "it's usually fine" rests on nothing.) Looking back at your own work does not sharpen it by itself. Feedback from outside, such as a colleague finding what you missed, does.
- **Trace.** Two versions of the same task, and what you changed between them and why. (First time you checked the headline figure. After the AI invented a quote, you now check quotes too.) No change and no reason is not a failure; it means we have no evidence either way.
- **Learning and performance.** Performance is how good the work is while you do it with AI. Learning is what you can do later without help. (A student who has AI write every essay hands in good essays and learns little.)
- **Satisficing.** Taking the first answer that looks good enough. AI makes it the default because its output always looks finished.
- **Domain knowledge.** You can only catch mistakes in something you know. (A strategist reading an AI's legal review cannot tell what it missed.)

## An exercise to show, not just describe

When someone asks for an exercise on judging what an AI agent did, a sketch like this makes the rules concrete. It is a design sketch for a 90-minute session, built from the design rules, not a tested programme. Say so.

**Same task, run twice.**
1. *(10 min)* Each person picks a real task from their own work that they know well, and writes down what a good result looks like. *Why: you can only judge in a domain you know, and the standard has to be yours.*
2. *(25 min)* They run it twice: once as a single question, once as an agent or workflow with a checkpoint that makes the AI list what it used. *Why: the contrast makes the choice between ways of working visible, and the checkpoint shows what the agent hid.*
3. *(20 min)* In pairs, one person who knows the domain looks for something wrong in the other's agent output. If the agent made no real error, the facilitator adds one realistic error. *Why: people learn to find errors by meeting them, and a planted one that is too obvious teaches people to hunt for plants.*
4. *(15 min)* Debrief: what did I check, what did I miss, what will I do differently next time? Each person writes one change. *Why: a short structured debrief improves the next attempt, and the written change is the reason a second run needs.*
5. *(10 min)* Plan the first real use at work: which task, when, and who knows. *Why: most of what people learn is lost at the first return to work.*

**What you would see if it worked.** People name one specific thing they now check that they did not before, and they can say why. A month later, a second run of the same task differs from the first, with a stated reason.

**What it does not do.** It does not show that anyone's work changed. That needs the second run, back at work.

## Order for a newcomer

1. One step of their own work, and the question "should AI help with this?"
2. The ways of working, with the running example.
3. Checking in proportion to the cost of a mistake.
4. Looking back: two runs and a debrief.
5. Quality dimensions, domain knowledge and the rest only if they ask or need them.

## Common misreadings to head off

- "More autonomy is better." It is not. A single question is often the right choice, and so is no AI.
- "Fluency is good prompting." Prompting is one competency, description. Judging the result and answering for it matter as much.
- "Reflection means asking the AI more questions." It means looking at your own practice: what worked, what you missed, what you will change.
- "Choosing no AI is a failure." It is a choice the framework counts as fluent when the step protects your judgement or costs more than it gives.
