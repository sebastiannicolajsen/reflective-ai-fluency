## 1. The 4D AI Fluency Framework

**Source.** Rick Dakan (Ringling College) and Joseph Feller (University College Cork), developed with Anthropic. Fluency is defined as working with AI effectively, efficiently, ethically and safely.

**Four competencies, each with three sub-competencies:**

| Competency | Sub-competencies | In plain words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delegation | Problem (goal) awareness; platform awareness; task delegation | Knowing what you want, what the AI can do, and how to split the work |
| Description | Product description; process description; performance description | Saying what you want, how the AI should work on it, and how it should behave with you |
| Discernment | Product discernment; process discernment; performance discernment | Judging the output, the AI's reasoning, and how the collaboration is going |
| Diligence | Creation diligence; transparency diligence; deployment diligence | Choosing systems responsibly, being honest about AI's role, standing behind what you release |

**Three modes of interaction**, not ranked: Automation (AI does a specific task on your instruction), Augmentation (you and AI think and create together), Agency (AI works independently on your behalf within boundaries you set). Dakan stresses matching the mode to the task.

**Two loops.** Delegation and Diligence form the strategic loop (whether and how responsibly to use AI); Description and Discernment form the tactical loop (the back and forth of the work itself).

**Teaching material** (from the Teaching AI Fluency course): four teaching sequences (linear, non-linear, focused, loop-based); assessment by outcome, process and reflection; handing in prompts and drafts with the final work; analysing AI's impact on one's own discipline. Four roles AI can play in finished work: consultant, tool builder, cocreator, medium.

**Licences.** The framework appears under different Creative Commons licences in different editions (CC BY-NC-ND in the v1.1 summary, CC BY-SA in the HEA edition, CC BY-NC-SA in the courses). Not blocking for internal working use; check before any commercial use.

## 2. The AI Fluency Index

Anthropic Education Report, 23 Feb 2026. 9,830 multi-turn Claude.ai conversations from one week in January 2026, coded for the 11 of the 24 behaviours that are visible in a chat.

- Iterating and refining appeared in 85.7% of conversations, and those conversations showed more of every other behaviour.
- When Claude produced finished artifacts (documents, code), people gave clearer instructions but checked less: fewer flagged missing context, checked facts or questioned reasoning.
- Limits stated by Anthropic: one platform, one week, likely early adopters, correlational, and the 13 behaviours outside the conversation, including some of the most consequential, were not measured.

## 3. How Reflective AI Fluency relates to 4D

| 4D | Reflective AI Fluency |
| --- | --- |
| Four competencies | Kept unchanged; asked as questions about one's own work |
| 24 behaviours | Kept as the behavioural core; all 24 treated as equal, not only the 11 measurable in chat |
| Three modes | Replaced by four ways of working sorted by who sets the path; Automation split into single interaction and workflow |
| Mode choice inside Delegation | Made explicit as a per-step choice, including no AI, with a choosing table and step-down and re-test triggers |
| Fluency as competence | Adds a quality axis: reflective quality and six dimensions, judged within a way of working |
| Implicit | Adds the domain precondition, tools for introspection, traces as evidence, satisficing as the default failure |

## 4. Critiques of 4D

- **Framing, Judging, Steering** (Apartsin & Aperstein, 2026, preprint): argues 4D's Description fuses specifying a task before generation with steering it afterwards, and proposes three separately assessable skills. Evidence so far comes from simulated learners only. Our answer: Description has two moments (framing and steering) in the manifest, and noticing and adjusting cover the monitoring and control the paper describes.
- **Measurement gap**: the Index measures behaviour presence, not whether the behaviour was appropriate. More fact-checking is not always better.
- **No levels**: 4D does not describe proficiency levels. We keep dimensions running weaker to stronger rather than levels.

## 5. Related AI frameworks

**OpenAI Academy.** Free courses in four tracks (applying AI at work, building with AI, leading adoption, teaching and learning). No explicit framework. Implicit habit loop: instruct, add context, review outputs, use responsibly. Implicit progression from task to workflow to agent to organisation. Uses "AI fluency" for the workforce and "AI literacy" for youth and policy; critical literacy appears mainly in its teen blueprint. Our ways of working borrow the task-to-agent orientation but drop the ladder.

**OECD and European Commission AI Literacy Framework** (final June 2026). For primary and secondary education. Four domains (engaging with AI, creating with AI, managing AI, shaping AI) and 19 competences combining knowledge, skills and attitudes; feeds PISA 2029. The May 2025 draft had 22 competences and a "designing AI" domain. Useful contrast: built from domains and competences, not levels, and not a measure.

**AI Assessment Scale** (Perkins, Furze, Roe and MacVaugh). Five levels of AI involvement in an assessment task, from no AI to full AI or AI exploration. The revised version is drawn as a circle to stop it being read as a hierarchy. **Two-lane approach** (University of Sydney): secure assessment of learning versus open assessment for learning where AI use is expected. Critique: stated AI rules are discursive; validity needs structural change to the task.

**Metacognitive demands of generative AI** (Tankelevitch et al., 2024). AI places demands on self-awareness and task decomposition when prompting, on well-adjusted confidence when evaluating output, and on flexibility about when and how to use AI. Maps to Description, Discernment and the choice of way of working.

**Levels of automation** (Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens, 2000). Automation varies by function (acquiring information, analysing, deciding, acting), so the level should be chosen per function, not per system. Supports choosing the way of working per step.

**Trust calibration** (Lee and See, 2004). Reliance should match the system's actual reliability; both over-reliance and under-reliance are failures. Basis for proportionate checking and calibration.

**Workflows versus agents** (Anthropic agent guidance). Workflows follow predefined paths; agents direct their own process. Basis for our workflow versus agency distinction.

**AI literacy scales.** Most published scales are self-report; very few test performance. None measures quality of practice in one's own work.

## 6. Learning theory behind the design rules

Each entry: what it is, what it does in our design, and its standing.

| Theory or finding | What it is | Role in our design | Standing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Constructive alignment (Biggs) | Outcomes, activities and assessment ask for the same verb | Cross-layer alignment check, not the method | Design logic; criticised when used as an audit tool |
| SOLO taxonomy (Biggs and Collis) | Grades the structure of a response, from one relevant point to generalising beyond it | Vocabulary for depth of reasoning in expert conversations; not a grading tool | Mixed rater reliability |
| Zone of proximal development, scaffolding, cognitive apprenticeship | Support for what a learner cannot yet do alone, faded as they gain skill; modelling, coaching, fading | Replaced in practice by expertise reversal; modelling and coaching kept | Hard to measure; components evidenced |
| Expertise reversal | Support that helps beginners hinders experienced learners | Rule 1: support per way of working | Meta-analytic support |
| Whole-task design (4C/ID) | Practice built on whole, varied real tasks with fading support | Rule 7: the practice architecture | Meta-analytic support, mostly education |
| Error management training | Encouraging errors and recovery during practice | Rule 8 | Meta-analytic support, mostly software |
| Behaviour modelling with mixed examples | Showing good and poor practice | Rule 9 | Meta-analytic support, workplace |
| Interleaving | Mixing similar categories improves discrimination | Rule 10, by analogy | Meta-analytic support, mostly category learning |
| Critical-thinking instruction | Discussion, authentic problems and mentoring | Rule 11 | Meta-analytic support, education |
| Debriefs and after-action reviews | Structured look-back after action | Rule 12 | Meta-analytic support |
| Deliberate reflection (Mamede and Schmidt) | Structured re-examination of a first judgement | Rule 13: complex cases only | Supported in medicine; some nulls |
| Calibration and metacognitive accuracy | Knowing how often one is right | Rule 14: needs outside feedback | Reflection does not reliably improve it |
| Feedback (Hattie and Timperley; Kluger and DeNisi) | Feedback on task and next step helps; on the self it can backfire | Rule 15 | Established |
| Psychological safety (Edmondson) | Team climate that allows admitting errors | Rule 16 | Established in teams |
| Implementation intentions | If-then plans for a specific action | Rule 17 | Meta-analytic support; smaller for complex behaviour |
| Transfer of training (Baldwin and Ford; Blume et al.) | Learner, design and work-environment factors | Rules 3 and 17; transfer layer | Meta-analytic, correlational |
| High-road transfer (Salomon and Perkins) | Deliberately abstracting a principle to carry it elsewhere | Transfer dimension | Established theory |
| Spacing | Revisiting after a gap improves retention | Rule 18 | Established for memory |
| Training evaluation (Kirkpatrick; Alliger et al.) | Reactions, learning, behaviour, results | Rule 19: traces, usefulness ratings | Enjoyment ratings predict little |
| Learning versus performance (Soderstrom and Bjork) | What people show during practice differs from what they retain | Rule 4 and section 3 of the foundation | Established |
| Self-regulated learning (Zimmerman; Sitzmann and Ely) | Forethought, performance, self-reflection | Background for reflective quality; goals and persistence matter more than self-monitoring | Established, correlational |
| Reflective practice (Schön); double-loop learning (Argyris and Schön) | Reflection in and on action; changing the strategy, not only the output | Noticing, reviewing, adjusting | Conceptual |
| Satisficing (Simon) | Settling for good enough under limits | The default AI creates; adaptive when stakes are low | Established concept |
| Semantic waves; concreteness fading | Moving between abstract principle and concrete case | Vocabulary only; rule 11 carries the idea | School and university evidence |
| Productive failure | Problem solving before instruction | Conceptual goals only | Meta-analytic support, school |
| Deliberate practice | Structured practice with feedback | "Practice with feedback" only | Explains little variance in professions |
| Andragogy (Knowles) | Assumptions about adult learners | Not used as justification | Largely untested |
| 70-20-10 | Claim that most learning is informal | Not used; the percentages lack evidence | Weak |
