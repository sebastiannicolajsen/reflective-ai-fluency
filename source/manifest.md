# Reflective AI Fluency: Manifest (draft 6)

Nov 4, 2026 · Sebastian Nicolajsen

Reflective AI Fluency is the ability to work with AI effectively, efficiently, ethically and safely in your own work, and to keep getting better at it. It builds on the 4D AI Fluency Framework by Rick Dakan and Joseph Feller (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) and adds what that framework leaves implicit: practising it in one's own context, choosing deliberately whether and how to use AI on each step, and the reflective quality that makes practice compound.

The framework has two axes. **Ways of working** describe who sets the path of the work, from a single interaction to agency. **Quality** describes how well and how reflectively the four competencies are performed. The two are linked: the more autonomy and the higher the stakes, the higher the quality needed to work safely. One precondition sits under both: you can only judge AI's work in a domain you know.

Evidence and arguments behind each claim are in the knowledge base. Background on the 4D framework and related frameworks is in the frameworks reference.

## What we believe

Fluency is something you do in your own work, and it shows in how you adapt. Eight statements set the stance.

1. **Fluency is behaviour, not knowledge.** Knowing the 4Ds matters only if it changes what people do with AI.
2. **Fluency lives in context.** It is built on your own tasks in your own domain, where you can judge the result.
3. **Fluency is awareness of AI inclusion and exclusion.** It means knowing where AI belongs in your work and where it does not, and why: because it helps, because it costs more than it gives, or because the step protects your expertise, judgement or responsibility.
4. **Checking matches the stakes, not the polish.** AI output looks finished whether or not it is right. Fluent people check heavily where an error would be costly and lightly where it would not.
5. **No single way of working is the goal.** Single interaction, collaboration, workflow and agency are all tools. Fluency is choosing the right one for each step and changing when it stops working. Often the right choice is the simplest one.
6. **Autonomy grows with capability and judgement.** As models improve, more can be handed over, but only as far as you can judge the results. Earlier choices are worth revisiting when capabilities change.
7. **Fluent people get better deliberately.** They look for what they are missing, notice, adjust and carry lessons forward. Using AI does not teach this by itself, so fluent people use tools that make their own practice visible to them: comparing two runs of the same task, short debriefs, and feedback from others on what they missed.
8. **Fluency ensures quality; it does not assume it.** It means choosing the way of working that gets the highest quality and making sure that quality holds. AI makes satisficing the default: the output always looks acceptable and the model rarely shows what is missing, so people accept the first good-enough result. Fluent people choose the way of working and the checks that deliver high quality, and keep looking for better.

## Precondition: knowing the domain

You can only catch errors you can recognise. Judging AI output in a domain you do not know is guessing, however careful the process looks. This is why fluency is built on your own work (statement 2), why discernment is weakest where domain knowledge is thin, and why handing more of the path to AI is only safe where you can judge the result (statement 6).

Domain knowledge is a precondition, not a dimension of fluency. A strong domain expert can still be a beginner with AI, and a fluent AI user is still exposed in an unfamiliar domain. When a step sits outside your domain, the fluent response is to lower autonomy, bring in someone who knows the domain, or treat the output as a draft to be verified.

## The four competencies

We keep Dakan and Feller's four competencies unchanged and express each as a question a person asks about their own work.

| Competency | In plain words | The question in your own work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delegation | Deciding whether, when and how to use AI, and in which way of working | Should AI help with this step, and how much should it do? |
| Description | Communicating goals, context, format and how the AI should work | Have I told it what I would tell a capable new colleague? |
| Discernment | Judging output, reasoning and the collaboration itself | How would I know if this is wrong, incomplete or misleading? |
| Diligence | Taking responsibility: choosing tools well, being open about AI's role, verifying before use | Can I stand behind this, and would I be comfortable saying how it was made? |

All four apply in every way of working; what changes is how they are performed.

Description has two moments: framing the task before the AI starts, and steering it as the work develops. Framing matters most in workflow and agency, where the path is set up front. Steering matters most in collaboration.

## Axis 1: Ways of working

The four ways are sorted by **who determines the path of the work**.

| Way of working | Who sets the path | What it looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single interaction | You, in one step | One question or instruction, one response, judged by you |
| Collaboration | You and the AI, step by step | Iterating together; the path emerges as you go |
| Workflow | You, in advance | You define the steps, inputs and checks; the AI executes them, often repeatedly |
| Agency | The AI, within boundaries | You set goals, boundaries and checkpoints; the AI chooses its own path |

**Workflow versus agency.** They differ in what you can inspect and in what they are for. A workflow exposes every step, so it suits repeatable, consistent and auditable work. Agency exposes goals, boundaries and results, and leaves the path in between to the AI, so it suits open-ended work whose path cannot be set in advance. How much that black box matters decides which fits.

**Relation to Dakan and Feller's modes.** Collaboration corresponds to their Augmentation, and Agency keeps their name. Their Automation (AI performs a specific task on your instruction) is split in two: a single interaction and a designed, repeatable workflow. We depart from the source here because the difference in who sets the path changes what Discernment and Diligence must do.

**No ladder.** The ways differ in difficulty and in the quality they require, not in value. A single interaction is often exactly right. The choice is made per step, so one person uses all four in a day, and some steps use none.

### Choosing the way of working

Decide per step. Autonomy is capped by how well you can check the result and what an undetected error would cost.

| Question | If no |
| --- | --- |
| Is it worth it, counting tokens, time and the effort of reviewing? | Do it yourself, or use a lighter way of working |
| Do I know this domain well enough to judge the output? | Lower autonomy, involve someone who does, or treat the output as a draft to verify |
| Is the goal clear? | Collaboration: explore with AI to find what good looks like |
| Can I check the output, quickly or at checkpoints? | Collaboration, or keep the step human |
| What does an undetected error cost, and is it reversible? | If high and irreversible: AI drafts, human decides |
| Is one exchange enough? | Collaboration, if the work needs several rounds |
| Can I fix the path in advance, and do I need to inspect it step by step? | No: collaboration, or agency with boundaries and checkpoints. Yes: workflow |
| Does it recur or have volume? | Don't invest in building a workflow; a one-off run is fine if the earlier answers are yes |

Cost grows with autonomy. A run with agency, or a workflow repeated at volume, can multiply token use and review effort many times over, so worth is asked again whenever the way of working changes.

Recurrence decides whether building a workflow is worth the effort. It does not decide whether delegating is allowed.

**Step-down triggers:** output drifts, stakes rise, or you can no longer say how you would know it is wrong.

**Re-test triggers:** the AI's capabilities change, or the cost of a way of working changes. A step that needed collaboration last year may now be safe to hand over.

The common mistake is starting with the most autonomous way before you know what good looks like. Exploring a new way is safest on a step from your own domain, where you can still judge the result.

## Axis 2: Quality

Quality is how well and how reflectively the four competencies are performed in a given way of working. It is assessed within a way of working, not across them.

### Reflective quality

Reflection is not a fifth competency or a separate loop; it is the quality with which all four are performed. A fluent person works like a fluent speaker: they notice a misunderstanding and rephrase mid-sentence, without stopping to run a procedure.

It shows in five ways:

- **Curiosity.** Looking for what a better result would be, even when the current one seems fine: asking for alternatives, comparing approaches, trying another way of working.
- **Noticing.** Recognising, during the work, that output is drifting, a check is missing or the way of working is wrong.
- **Reviewing.** Looking back after the work: what did I just do, what worked, what would I change?
- **Adjusting.** Changing the description, the check or the way of working in response, including trying a different way on a task you already know well, and re-testing earlier choices when the AI's capabilities change.
- **Carrying forward.** Turning what worked into reusable practice (a prompt, a checklist, a workflow) and revising it when it fails.

Noticing happens during the work; reviewing happens after it. While fluency is developing, both are often deliberate and visible; with fluency they become part of how the work is done.

Reflection improves what people do more reliably than it improves how well they know their own judgement. Looking back makes the next attempt better, but it rarely tells you how good your checking actually is. That second part needs something outside your own view: an error someone else found, a check that missed, a comparison with what happened later. Calibration (below) depends on it.

The failure mode is **satisficing**: accepting the first good-enough result instead of ensuring the best quality you can reach. With AI it is the default: the output always looks acceptable, the model rarely signals what is missing, and good-enough use already saves time, so there is no felt reason to ask whether better exists. The costs are errors no one sees and value no one reaches. Curiosity is how you test whether better exists.

### Tools for introspection

Fluent people make their own practice visible to themselves. The framework relies on four tools:

- **Two runs of the same task.** Compare them and ask: "What did you change since last time, and why?"
- **A short debrief after the work.** What worked, what did the AI get wrong, what will I do differently next time.
- **Outside feedback.** Someone else, or a later outcome, shows what your checks missed.
- **Your own conversations as material.** Reading back how you worked with AI against the behaviours listed below.

### Observable traces

A trace is a difference between two points in time on the same task, with a reason. It shows that practice changed and why. It does not by itself show how reflective someone is; it is the evidence we can see.

- A prompt, check or workflow that changed between versions, with a stated reason.
- A check added after an error was caught.
- A way of working switched when output drifted or stakes rose.
- Alternatives requested after an acceptable answer.
- A reusable artifact revised after it failed.
- The person can name what the AI got wrong last time and what they do differently now.

**Rule:** no change and no reason means no evidence, not failure. A stable practice that still works is not a lack of reflection.

### Dimensions of quality

Quality is described as dimensions that run from weaker to stronger, not as levels. They apply within each way of working.

| Dimension | Weaker | Stronger |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reliability | Errors are found by others later, or never | Errors are found by you, during the work |
| Proportionate checking | The same check whatever the risk, or none | The check matches what an error would cost |
| Calibration | Confidence in your own judgement and in the AI does not match how often each is right | Confidence matches how often each is right, and is adjusted when evidence shows otherwise |
| Reasoning | Cannot give reasons for the way of working, check or prompt chosen | Gives reasons, and says what would change the choice |
| Adaptation | Same practice on the same task whatever the output or conditions | Practice on the same task changes when the output or conditions change |
| Transfer | What was learned stays with one task | What was learned carries to new tasks and to other ways of working |

Notes on reading the dimensions:

- **Reliability counts errors found, not errors avoided.** Fluent people often meet more failures than beginners, because they push further, but their failures are visible and recoverable. A clean-looking record is not evidence of reliability.
- **Calibration sits under proportionate checking.** You can only match checks to stakes if you know how often you and the AI are right. Reflection alone does not sharpen it; outside feedback does.
- **Adaptation and transfer differ in what changes.** Adaptation is about the same task under new conditions. Transfer is about a new task or a new way of working.
- **Reliability differs from the others.** The others describe how you work; reliability shows whether the work delivers.

### How the axes link

- **Required quality rises with autonomy and with stakes.** Autonomy decides how late you would see an error: in collaboration you catch it as it happens, in agency only at checkpoints. Stakes decide what an undetected error costs. The same weak check that is harmless in a single interaction is dangerous in a workflow others rely on.
- **Curiosity motivates the move, Discernment decides whether it is safe.** Curiosity drives people toward more autonomous ways of working. Without the ability to judge output, that only widens the amount of unchecked work.
- **Stepping down is part of quality.** Being able to run an agent is a way of working. Knowing when to step back from it is quality.
- **Edge case: more human involvement is not always safer.** Where the AI is reliably better at a step and its output can be checked, adding human judgement can make the result worse. Calibration decides which case you are in.

The axes give four positions, and the top right is the main risk:

| | Lower autonomy (single interaction, collaboration) | Higher autonomy (workflow, agency) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lower quality** | One-shot answers, accepted unchecked | Workflows or agents running with no real checks |
| **Higher quality** | Sharp interrogation of answers and iterations | Autonomy matched by checks, boundaries and revision |

## The behaviours we look for

We use the 24 behavioural indicators of the 4D framework as the core and apply the reflective quality as a lens across them. Anthropic's AI Fluency Index measured the 11 that can be coded from a chat transcript. The other 13 matter as much: several of them, such as being open about AI's role and considering the consequences of sharing the work, are among the most consequential parts of fluency, and they happen around the work rather than in the conversation. The framework treats all 24 as equal.

We also watch for defaults that go unexamined: satisficing (accepting the first acceptable output on work that matters), accepting output passively, delegating vaguely, trusting polished output without checking, judging output in a domain you do not know, and the reverse, using AI where it is not worth the cost.

## Fluency in your own work

Fluency is always fluency in something: it shows in how a person handles the steps of their own work.

For any step of their work, a fluent person can answer four questions:

| Question | Shows |
| --- | --- |
| Does AI belong in this step and, if so, in which way of working and at what cost? | Delegation, including awareness of exclusion |
| How would I know the output is wrong, and do I know this domain well enough to tell? | Discernment and the domain precondition |
| What role does AI play in the result (consultant, tool builder, cocreator, medium)? | Diligence and openness about AI's role |
| Is this the best way to get high quality here, and how do I ensure it? | Reflective quality |

Over time, the answers change: new ways of working, new checks, steps joined into larger pieces of work. That change is how growing fluency shows.

## What this is not

Reflective AI Fluency is not a tool course, a prompt-technique catalogue or a ranking of people. It does not treat automation, autonomy or any particular way of working as the goal, and it does not certify knowledge about AI.
