# Reflective AI Fluency: Learning Design Foundation (draft 2)

Nov 4, 2026 · Sebastian Nicolajsen

This document states how exercises, learning goals and learning journeys for Reflective AI Fluency should be designed, why, and how strong the evidence for each reason is. It is written against manifest draft 6. It does not design a programme.

Each rule points to an argument in the knowledge base (IDs such as L07). The knowledge base holds the full evidence, sources, limits and theory names. This document holds the rules and the plain reasons.

## 1. How the skill uses this document

1. **Ask before judging.** Before giving feedback, the skill asks what the design is meant to change, who it is for, which ways of working it covers, and which real task it uses. It also asks whether the peer is an experienced facilitator or new to learning design, and adjusts how much structure it offers (rule 1 applies to the skill's users too).
2. **Lead with what matters most.** It gives the two or three points that most change the design, not a full checklist.
3. **Quote the design.** It points to the text it is reacting to.
4. **Give reasons in plain words, and arguments when needed.** Every point carries its plain reason. The skill brings in the evidence behind a reason when the peer challenges it, when a choice is contested, or when the peer asks why. It names a theory or study only when the peer asks for it or works at a level where the name helps. It never opens with theory names.
5. **State confidence.** Each rule carries a rating:
   - **Established:** peer-reviewed, replicated or meta-analytic evidence, possibly from a neighbouring field.
   - **Plausible:** strong evidence from a neighbouring setting, not tested on AI-assisted work.
   - **Preprint:** AI-specific evidence not yet peer-reviewed.
   - **Our judgement:** a design choice with no direct evidence. The skill says so.
6. **Name one strength only when it is specific.** No total score, no default praise. AI reviewers of learning designs tend to over-praise and agree with experts only moderately (K01), so the skill treats its own judgements on alignment and depth as tentative.
7. **Return the decision.** It ends with a question the designer decides.
8. **Behave fluently itself.** It shows its reasoning, says what it could not check, and prompts the peer to look back at what they changed.

## 2. What the design has to produce

The manifest defines the outcomes. A design is good when it develops:

- **Behaviour in the person's own work**, not knowledge about AI.
- **A per-step choice of way of working** (single interaction, collaboration, workflow, agency), including the choice to use no AI.
- **The six quality dimensions:** reliability, proportionate checking, calibration, reasoning, adaptation, transfer.
- **Reflective quality and the habit of using tools for introspection**: comparing runs, debriefing, seeking outside feedback.
- **Quality that matches autonomy and stakes.**

It rests on one precondition: people can only practise judging AI output in a domain they know.

## 3. Learning and performance

A design can raise two different things, and they do not always move together.

- **Performance** is the quality of the work while the person does it, with AI and with the support the design offers.
- **Learning** is the lasting change in what the person decides and judges later, on their own work, after the support is gone.

From a constructivist view these should be the same, because people learn through what they do. With AI they come apart: when the AI does the thinking, the work gets better while the person's own thinking does not happen. In one field study, students with unguarded AI access performed far better during practice and worse on a later unaided exam; a guarded version avoided the harm (L04). So the split is a constructivist point, not a departure from it: learning happens only where the learner does the constructing.

For AI fluency both matter, because the target behaviour is itself performance with AI. A design should say which one each element serves, and the evidence of learning should come from a later run (a trace), not from the assisted output.

## 4. The seven layers

A design answers one question per layer. Two checks run across all of them (section 6).

| Layer | The question the designer answers | What it takes from the framework |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0. Starting point | Who is this for, how experienced are they in each way of working, and do they know the domain? | Way of working × dimension; domain precondition |
| 1. Outcome | What will people do differently, on which step, in which way of working? Is it learning or performance? | Behaviour in own work |
| 2. Evidence | What trace will show it, and could AI produce that trace alone? | Traces, two runs |
| 3. Practice | What do people actually do to build it? | Ways of working, dimensions |
| 4. Feedback and reflection | How do they find out what to change, and how well they check? | Reflective quality, calibration, tools for introspection |
| 5. Transfer and environment | What makes the first real use at work happen? | Own context, recurrence |
| 6. Evaluation | How will we know, later, that work changed? | Traces over time |

## 5. Design rules by layer

Each rule has: **Why** (plain reason), **Evidence** (plain summary, rating, knowledge-base ID), **Does not hold when**, and **Skill asks**.

### Layer 0: Starting point

**Rule 1. Match support to experience in this way of working, and withdraw it as people gain skill.**
- **Why:** Step-by-step help speeds up people new to a way of working and slows down people who already know it. Experience belongs to a way of working, so one person can be expert in collaboration and new to workflows.
- **Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 60 experiments found that beginners gained from high support and experienced learners did better with less. Established; mostly student samples. (L01)
- **Does not hold when:** Seniority is used as a stand-in for experience. When unsure, give support: holding it back from a beginner costs more than giving it to an expert.
- **Skill asks:** "Who in the group already uses workflows well, and who is new to them?"

**Rule 2. Put judgement practice inside a domain the participants know.**
- **Why:** You can only catch errors you can recognise. Practising discernment on unfamiliar material trains guessing.
- **Evidence:** Expertise is domain-bound; novices miss failures they cannot see; experts catch more errors when they work in their own field and with a structured way of looking. Established for expertise in general; preprint for AI-specific findings. (L02)
- **Does not hold when:** The goal is to practise recognising the limits of one's own domain knowledge. Then an unfamiliar domain is the point, and the exercise should make that explicit.
- **Skill asks:** "Do participants know this material well enough to tell when the AI is wrong?"

### Layer 1: Outcome

**Rule 3. State each outcome as a behaviour in a named way of working, including choosing not to use AI.**
- **Why:** An outcome phrased as understanding can be met without any change in how someone works. A named way of working says what the person decides and where, and "no AI" is a decision as much as "agent".
- **Evidence:** Follows from the framework. Workplace training research finds that judgement-based skills transfer when conditions at work support them. Starting from observable behaviour is a standard practitioner method without controlled tests. Plausible; outcome wording is our judgement. (L03)
- **Does not hold when:** A short awareness session only aims to inform. Label it as awareness, not fluency.
- **Skill asks:** "What will the participant do differently, on which real step, in which way of working?"

**Rule 4. Say whether each element aims at learning or at performance.**
- **Why:** AI can lift the work during the exercise while the person learns nothing they keep (section 3). Both aims are legitimate, but a design that confuses them measures the wrong thing.
- **Evidence:** Field and lab studies show assisted performance rising while later unaided performance stays flat or falls, and guarded designs avoiding the drop. Established for the effect; the design rule is our judgement. (L04)
- **Does not hold when:** The element is purely a tool introduction where only performance matters.
- **Skill asks:** "Is this step meant to make today's work better, or to change how they work next month?"

### Layer 2: Evidence

**Rule 5. The evidence of learning must be a trace of the person's own work and judgement that AI cannot produce alone.**
- **Why:** If an agent can finish the exercise convincingly, finishing it proves nothing. A rule that only states how AI may be used does not change what people do; the task has to make the use visible.
- **Evidence:** In a field test, AI-written exam answers went undetected in 94% of cases and outscored most real students. Assessment research argues that stated rules without structural change leave validity unprotected. Established for the problem; preprint for specific patterns. (L05)
- **Does not hold when:** The task is practice, not evidence. Practice tasks need not be AI-proof. Evidence locked down so tightly that it hides how AI was used defeats the purpose.
- **Skill asks:** "If someone gave this exercise to an agent, what would we still see of their own judgement?" The skill also runs a generic prompt through the exercise and reports what came back.

**Rule 6. Ask for two runs of the same task and the reason for what changed.**
- **Why:** One finished output can be made by AI. A difference between two attempts, with a reason, is harder to fake and shows that practice changed.
- **Evidence:** Thin. Process evidence resists AI better than a single artefact, but nobody has validated it as a measure of AI-practice quality, and reflections can be written by AI. A trace shows changed behaviour, not reflective capacity. Our judgement. (L06)
- **Does not hold when:** The person was already working well and has no reason to change. No change and no reason means no evidence, not failure.
- **Skill asks:** "What would someone actually see differing between the two runs?"

### Layer 3: Practice

**Rule 7. Build practice on whole tasks from the person's own work, and vary them.**
- **Why:** Judgement develops when it is practised whole and in real conditions. Invented tasks teach invented judgement.
- **Evidence:** Programmes built around whole tasks showed large gains, mostly in education and technical training. Critical-thinking instruction gains grew with authentic problems, discussion and mentoring. Established for education; plausible for consulting. (L07)
- **Does not hold when:** Someone lacks a basic mechanic, such as setting up a workflow tool; a short drill first is fine. Confidentiality can rule out real tasks; then use realistic ones the person recognises.
- **Skill asks:** "Which real task from these people's week could carry the exercise?"

**Rule 8. Let people meet and fix AI errors on purpose, with a clear signal of what went wrong.**
- **Why:** People who practise finding and recovering from errors handle new situations better than people who only practise correct procedures.
- **Evidence:** A meta-analysis of 24 studies found a moderate advantage that was largest on structurally new tasks. Established, mostly on software tasks. (L08)
- **Does not hold when:** Feedback is unclear, which describes much agentic work: add a checkpoint that reveals the error. Planted errors that are too easy teach people to hunt for plants.
- **Skill asks:** "How will participants know what they missed?"

**Rule 9. Show good and poor examples of AI use, including a case where using no AI was the right call.**
- **Why:** Contrast makes the decision visible. Good examples alone teach imitation. AI use is not always the better choice: on average, human-AI combinations did worse than the better of the two alone.
- **Evidence:** Workplace training with both good and poor models transferred better. The human-AI finding comes from a meta-analysis of 106 experiments. Established for both; the design implication is our judgement. (L09)
- **Does not hold when:** True beginners see a poor example before any good one (our judgement).
- **Skill asks:** "Is there a case where leaving AI out is the right answer, and does the exercise reward that choice?"

**Rule 10. Mix decisions between ways of working in the same exercise.**
- **Why:** The hard part is telling which way fits a step, and that is only practised when the options sit side by side.
- **Evidence:** Mixing similar categories improves the ability to tell them apart, with no reliable benefit for learning from text. Plausible analogy; nothing tests it for ways of working. (L10)
- **Does not hold when:** A way of working is new to the group, or the content is reading material.
- **Skill asks:** "Would it help to mix steps where different ways of working compete? This is a reasoned bet."

**Rule 11. Use discussion of real cases, led by someone who works fluently in that way.**
- **Why:** Saying why you chose a way of working or a check, and hearing it challenged, builds reasoning.
- **Evidence:** Discussion, authentic problems and mentoring each improved critical thinking, and the combination helped most. Established for education; plausible for consulting. That the lead should be fluent in the way of working is our judgement. (L11)
- **Does not hold when:** One voice dominates the group.
- **Skill asks:** "Who leads the discussion, and have they used this way of working on real work?"

### Layer 4: Feedback and reflection

**Rule 12. End every practice round with a structured debrief that names one change for next time.**
- **Why:** A few minutes of structured looking back reliably improves the next attempt, and it produces the stated reason that rule 6 needs.
- **Evidence:** Debriefs improved performance by about 25% over controls in a meta-analysis of 46 samples, more when facilitated and structured. Established. (L12)
- **Does not hold when:** The debrief is an unstructured "how did it feel" or turns into blame.
- **Skill asks:** "What will the debrief ask, and who records the change?"

**Rule 13. Use structured reflection on complex outputs, not on everything.**
- **Why:** Stopping to test a first judgement helps on hard cases. On easy ones it costs time and adds little.
- **Evidence:** In medical diagnosis, deliberate reflection helped on complex cases and not on simple ones; guided reflection showed small gains overall and some null results. Established in medicine; plausible for AI work. (L13)
- **Does not hold when:** The output is simple or routine.
- **Skill asks:** "Is this output complex enough to justify a reflection step?"

**Rule 14. Add outside feedback so people can calibrate how well they and the AI judge.**
- **Why:** Reflecting makes people perform better. It does not reliably tell them how good their checking is, or how far to trust the AI. That needs evidence from outside their own view.
- **Evidence:** Deliberate reflection raised diagnostic accuracy without improving calibration; feedback on AI use changed behaviour without improving metacognitive accuracy; confidence in AI predicted less checking. Established in medicine; preprint and self-report for AI. Nothing reviewed reliably trains calibration. (L14)
- **Does not hold when:** Not applicable; this rule states a limit.
- **Skill asks:** "How will participants learn whether their checks matched what an error would have cost, and whether their trust in the AI was right?"

**Rule 15. Give feedback on the work and the next step, not on the person.**
- **Why:** Feedback about the person tends to backfire; feedback about the task and what to do next tends to help.
- **Evidence:** Feedback research finds large average effects with wide variation, and more than a third of feedback interventions lowered performance, especially those aimed at the self. Established. (L15)
- **Does not hold when:** Not applicable.
- **Skill asks:** "Does the feedback say where they are going, how they are doing, and what to try next?"

**Rule 16. Make it safe to report what the AI got wrong, and say who hears it.**
- **Why:** People who fear looking careless hide AI errors, and hidden errors cannot be learned from.
- **Evidence:** Team research links psychological safety to learning behaviour. Established in general; no AI-specific study found. (L16)
- **Does not hold when:** Errors involve client harm or confidentiality; then the reporting route must be set before the session.
- **Skill asks:** "Where can people say what the AI got wrong, and who hears it?"

### Layer 5: Transfer and environment

**Rule 17. Plan the first real use before people leave: when, on which task, with whose support.**
- **Why:** Most learning is lost at the first return to work. A concrete if-then plan and a manager who expects the new behaviour make the first use likely.
- **Evidence:** If-then plans had a moderate average effect across 642 tests, larger when people were motivated. Transfer of judgement-based skills depends strongly on a supportive environment. Established; environment findings correlational. (L17)
- **Does not hold when:** The behaviour is a whole style of judgement. If-then plans suit discrete actions, such as "if I hand a step to an agent, I first write the checkpoint".
- **Skill asks:** "What is the first real task where they will use this, and does their manager know?"

**Rule 18. Bring the same task type back after a gap, so that a second run is possible.**
- **Why:** The two-run trace needs a second run, and spacing helps retention.
- **Evidence:** Spacing is well established in memory research; evidence for spaced judgement training in professionals is thinner. Plausible. (L18)
- **Does not hold when:** The task type will not recur in the person's work.
- **Skill asks:** "When does this task come up again, and how will the second run be captured?"

### Layer 6: Evaluation

**Rule 19. Judge by traces of changed work over time. Collect usefulness ratings if you collect ratings at all.**
- **Why:** Liking a session says little about whether work changed.
- **Evidence:** Enjoyment ratings relate weakly to later behaviour; usefulness ratings relate more strongly. No validated measure of AI-practice quality exists, and most AI-literacy scales are self-report. Established. (L19)
- **Does not hold when:** A quick usefulness rating is fine as an early signal.
- **Skill asks:** "What trace will show the change a month later, and who sees it?"

## 6. Cross-layer checks

**Alignment.** The outcome, the practice and the evidence ask for the same behaviour, and the AI-use condition is stated together with the evidence of how AI was used. Patterns to name:
- The outcome says "judge" or "choose", the task only asks people to list.
- People are assessed on something they never practised.
- AI could complete the task without the target judgement.
- The AI-use condition does not match the outcome: a task that bans AI cannot evidence an outcome about using it, and a task that allows anything cannot evidence an outcome about knowing when to leave it out.
- A change over time is assessed from one artefact.
- Learning is claimed from assisted performance.
- Activities serve no outcome, or outcomes have no practice or evidence.

Rating: plausible. A coherence check, not a tested method; used as an audit it risks box-ticking. (L20)

**AI stress-test.** Run a generic prompt through the exercise. If the result would satisfy it, the exercise tests the AI. Re-run it when models change. Rating: established for the problem. (L05)

## 7. How the rules map to the quality dimensions

| Dimension | Design elements that plausibly build it | Rating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reliability | Errors on purpose (8); poor examples (9); reflection on complex outputs (13); own domain (2) | Established in neighbouring fields; analogy for AI |
| Proportionate checking | Cost-of-error cases (our judgement); outside feedback (14) | Thin |
| Calibration | Outside feedback comparing confidence with outcomes (14) | Thin; nothing reliably trains it |
| Reasoning | Discussion of real cases (11); debriefs (12) | Established in education and teams |
| Adaptation | Varied whole tasks (7); errors on purpose (8); mixing decisions (10) | Established for lab tasks; plausible for AI |
| Transfer | Own-work tasks (7); first-use plans (17); return after a gap (18) | Established; environment findings correlational |

## 8. Limits: where this is our judgement

The skill says so plainly when these come up:

- How to train the per-step choice of way of working.
- How to train proportionate checking and calibration.
- How to train supervision of agents.
- How to diagnose starting points per way of working, and how to sequence the ways.
- Whether the six dimensions work as a set.
- Whether two-run traces measure reflection or only show changed behaviour.
- How people decide whether AI is worth its cost in tokens, time and review effort.

## 9. What this replaces

For the designer: constructive alignment moved from spine to cross-layer check; SOLO and semantic waves are available as vocabulary for expert conversations, not as grading tools; the zone of proximal development gave way to the evidence on support that helps beginners and hinders experienced learners; 70-20-10 percentages, andragogy as justification and deliberate practice beyond "practice with feedback" are dropped; productive failure is kept only for conceptual goals. Reasons are in the knowledge base (L21).
